## What this page teaches

This page shows you how to find and read the Bloom educational
content inside Terrarium, the macOS application that hosts Grain's
development tools. You will learn where the Lessons tab lives, how
to move between sections, and how to reach the network demos that
use a fourteen-galaxy test network.

## Opening the Lessons tab

When you launch Terrarium, the window opens with a sidebar on the
left and a content area on the right. The sidebar lists the tabs
available to you. One of them is labeled **Bloom**. Click it, and
the content area fills with the first page of Grain's educational
platform.

If the sidebar is hidden, press Cmd+0 to bring it back.

## What the Lessons tab holds

The Bloom tab organizes its content into seven sections, each
separated by a header line. Here is what you will find, in order:

1. **Curriculum** -- ten stages that walk you from "What is an
   operating system?" through cryptographic identity, Glow
   programming, and the full Grain stack. Each stage builds on
   the one before it.

2. **Lineage teachings** -- seven pages tracing the ideas Grain
   inherits from its ancestors: Unix, Urbit, seL4, TigerBeetle,
   the Zig language, biocyclic vegan agriculture, and the Vedic
   tradition that gives Grain its naming sense.

3. **Ecology** -- pages about the living systems that Grain is
   built to serve: soil health, water cycles, and how a personal
   server helps a grower keep their own records.

4. **Guides** -- operational documents for people who run Grain
   in their daily work: a staff guide, a network operator's
   handbook, and a contributor's introduction.

5. **Essays** -- four longer pieces exploring the ideas behind
   Grain's design: why personal servers matter, why bounded
   software serves a farm, and what it means to build tools
   with devotion.

6. **Tutorials** -- four hands-on walkthroughs that use the
   Terrarium application itself as the learning surface: how to
   run a RISC-V program, how to watch a framebuffer, and how to
   read UART output.

7. **Network demos** -- nine walkthroughs that explore a
   fourteen-galaxy test network called a Puddle. These demos
   cover identity, encrypted messaging, consensus, token
   transfers, and social networking -- all using placeholder
   galaxy names that can never reach a real ship.

## Moving between pages

At the bottom of each page, you will find **Next** and
**Previous** buttons. Next moves you forward through the current
section and then into the next section. Previous takes you back.

You can also scroll the list in the sidebar to jump directly to
any page. The sidebar shows every page's title, so you can find
what you need without reading everything in order.

## Finding the network demos

The network demos appear at the end of the Lessons tab, after the
tutorials section. A separator header reads **Pond/Puddle Network
Demos**. The first page in this section is the Galaxy Registry,
which introduces the fourteen placeholder galaxies. From there,
the demos walk through progressively deeper layers of the network.

The fourteen galaxies are named for garden plants: `~sunflower`,
`~rosemary`, `~lavender`, `~basil`, `~chamomile`, `~oregano`,
`~thyme`, `~sage`, `~mint`, `~fennel`, `~marjoram`, `~tarragon`,
`~chive`, and `~dill`. Each name uses segments of four to nine
letters, which makes them structurally impossible to confuse with
a real Urbit address. You can copy any of them safely.

## Reading at your own pace

The Bloom tab is not a test. There is no timer, no score, and no
wrong order. If you are a grower who wants to understand how your
records are kept safe, start with the curriculum. If you are a
developer who wants to see the network in action, skip ahead to
the demos. If you are curious about where these ideas come from,
read the lineage teachings.

Every page was written to be understood on its own, even if you
have not read the pages before it.

## Something to try

Open the Lessons tab and use Next to walk from the first
curriculum stage through to the Galaxy Registry. Count how many
section headers you pass. Notice that the content changes tone
as it moves from teaching concepts to showing the network in
action -- that shift is the boundary between the curriculum and
the demos, and it is there on purpose.
