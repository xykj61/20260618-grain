## What this page teaches

A farm needs more than soil and seeds. It needs tools, packaging, building
materials, and a way to move its harvest to the people who eat it. This page
describes how a biocyclic vegan farm thinks about its supply chain -- where
materials come from, how products reach their destination, and how Grain's
records make that chain visible and auditable.

## What a farm buys

A working farm purchases things from outside its boundary:

- **Seeds and propagation material.** Seed varieties suited to the climate,
  soil, and market. A farm building its own seed library reduces this over
  time, yet some purchased seed is normal.

- **Tools and equipment.** Hand tools, irrigation components, harvest bins,
  a tractor or cultivation equipment on larger farms. These last years when
  maintained.

- **Packaging.** Boxes, bags, containers for getting produce to market.
  Reusable packaging -- returnable crates, refillable containers -- reduces
  this line item over time.

- **Building materials.** Greenhouse structures, fencing, storage buildings,
  compost infrastructure. A farm that builds with local timber and recycled
  materials keeps this closer to home.

- **Energy inputs.** Fuel for equipment, electricity for irrigation pumps
  and cold storage. The energy page covers how a farm can produce much of
  this itself.

## What a farm sells

The harvest moves outward through one or more channels:

- **Direct sale.** Farmers' markets, farm stands, community-supported
  agriculture (CSA) subscriptions. The shortest chain: grower to eater,
  no intermediary.

- **Wholesale.** Selling to restaurants, grocery cooperatives, or
  distributors. Larger volume, lower margin, and somebody else handles the
  last mile to the customer.

- **Value-added products.** Dried herbs, preserved foods, seed packets,
  compost for garden use. These extend the harvest season and use produce
  that would otherwise go to waste.

## What the records show

A Grain machine tracks both sides of the chain:

- **Purchase records.** What was bought, from whom, when, at what cost.
  Over years, these reveal which suppliers are reliable and which inputs
  the farm is successfully replacing with its own production.

- **Sales records.** What was sold, to whom, when, at what price. These
  feed directly into the farm's financial picture -- the Dimeroll ledger
  that tracks revenue, expenses, and the direction the farm is heading.

- **Inventory.** What is on hand -- seeds in storage, tools in the shed,
  packaging in the barn, harvest in the cooler. Knowing what you have is
  how you know what you need.

- **Traceability.** Which field produced which harvest, which harvest went
  to which customer. When a customer asks where their food came from, the
  answer is specific: this field, this variety, harvested on this date.

Each record enters Mantra as an append-only entry. A season's worth of
purchase and sales records, sealed by Amphora, gives the farm a complete
picture of what flowed in and what flowed out -- the supply chain made
visible.

## Something to try

Pick one meal and trace where each ingredient came from. The rice, the
vegetables, the oil, the spices. How many steps did each one take to reach
your plate? How many of those steps could you name?

A short supply chain is one where the grower and the eater can see each
other. The records a Grain machine keeps are what make that visibility
possible at a scale beyond a single handshake.
