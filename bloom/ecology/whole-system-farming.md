## What this page teaches

A farm is a living system. This page describes how to think about a farm as a
whole -- soil, plants, water, sunlight, and the cycles that connect them --
and how Grain's technology helps a farmer tend that whole system over years
and decades.

## Helen Atthowe and perennial systems

Helen Atthowe is a researcher who spent thirty years studying what happens
when a farm is managed as a complete system rather than as a collection of
separate fields. Her work, conducted primarily in Montana, demonstrated
several things that matter to anyone growing food:

1. **Soil organic matter rises over time** when cover crops, mulches, and
   perennial plantings are maintained consistently. Her fields showed organic
   matter increasing year over year, measurably.

2. **External inputs decrease as soil health improves.** A farm that builds
   its own fertility through composting, cover crops, and perennial root
   systems needs less fertilizer, less pest management, and less irrigation
   with each passing year.

3. **Biodiversity is functional infrastructure.** Multiple species of plants,
   insects, and soil organisms form a network where each member contributes
   something. Pollinators visit diverse plantings. Predatory insects manage
   pest populations. Deep-rooted perennials bring nutrients up from layers
   that annual crops cannot reach.

The insight is that a farm managed as a whole system grows more resilient over
time, while a farm managed as a set of isolated operations grows more
dependent on external support.

## Biocyclic vegan agriculture

Biocyclic vegan agriculture is a formal discipline with its own standards,
recognized internationally. Its core principles:

- **Plant-based fertility.** Soil is built with plant-based compost, cover
  crops, mulches, and green manures. Animal agriculture is excluded from the
  cycle, both as a practice and as a fertility source.

- **Closed nutrient loops.** What the farm grows, the farm returns. Crop
  residues become compost. Compost becomes soil. Soil grows the next crop.
  The goal is a cycle that holds more nutrients inside the farm boundary with
  each turn.

- **Biodiversity at every level.** Diversity of crops, diversity of varieties
  within each crop, diversity of non-crop plantings (hedgerows, pollinator
  strips, cover crop mixes), and diversity of soil life. Each kind of
  diversity serves a function.

- **Humus building.** Humus is the stable, long-lasting fraction of soil
  organic matter. Building humus is the central long-term investment of
  biocyclic vegan agriculture, because humus holds water, holds nutrients,
  supports soil structure, and sequesters carbon. Biocyclic humus soil --
  a compost product developed within this discipline -- is a specific
  technique for accelerating humus formation.

## What the machine records

A Grain machine on a biocyclic vegan farm keeps the log that makes
whole-system thinking practical over years:

- **Soil tests** -- organic matter percentage, nutrient levels, pH, microbial
  activity -- recorded into Mantra with the date, the field, and the lab that
  ran the test. Five years of these records show the direction the soil is
  moving.

- **Planting records** -- what was planted, when, where, which variety, from
  which seed source. These records make rotation planning possible and let a
  farmer learn which varieties thrive in their particular soil and climate.

- **Harvest records** -- yield by field, by variety, by date. Over time, these
  reveal patterns that a single season cannot show.

- **Compost records** -- inputs (which crop residues, which cover crops), the
  composting process (temperature, turning schedule, duration), and the
  finished product's nutrient content. Composting is a biological process
  that improves with attention, and attention needs records.

- **Water records** -- rainfall, irrigation applied, soil moisture readings.
  On a farm building soil health, water-holding capacity rises over the
  years, and the records prove it.

Each of these records enters Mantra as an append-only log entry and is sealed
by Amphora. Three years from now, five years from now, ten years from now,
the farmer can look back at records they know are exactly as written and make
decisions grounded in their own land's own history.

## Something to try

Visit a farmers' market and talk to a grower. Ask what they do with their
crop residues after harvest. Ask whether they use cover crops and, if so,
which species. Ask whether they compost and how.

The answers tell you how close that farm is to a closed-loop system. A grower
who composts their own residues and grows their own fertility is practicing
whole-system farming, whether they call it that or have any idea about
biocyclic vegan agriculture. The principle is older than the name.
