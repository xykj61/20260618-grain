## What this page teaches

Water flows through a farm the way blood flows through a body. This page
describes how a small farm can manage its water as a closed loop -- catching
rain, using it wisely, treating what leaves, and recording the whole cycle so
the farm grows more water-wise with each season.

## Catching rain

Rainwater that falls on a roof, a path, or a hardscape can be captured and
stored. A simple system consists of:

- **Collection surfaces** -- roofs are the most common. A clean metal or tile
  roof sheds water that is suitable for irrigation and, with filtration, for
  many other uses.
- **Gutters and downspouts** that channel the water.
- **Storage tanks** (cisterns) that hold it until it is needed.

The amount of water a roof can capture is straightforward arithmetic: the roof
area in square feet, multiplied by the rainfall in inches, multiplied by 0.623
gives gallons. A 1,000-square-foot roof in an area receiving 30 inches of rain
per year captures roughly 18,700 gallons -- enough to irrigate a substantial
garden through a dry spell.

Mantra records the rainfall, the tank levels, and the irrigation applied, so
the farmer sees how much the rain covers and how much supplemental water is
needed. Over years, these records show whether the farm's water-holding
capacity -- both in tanks and in soil -- is growing.

## Soil as a sponge

Healthy soil holds water. The organic matter that biocyclic vegan agriculture
builds into the soil over the years acts as a sponge -- each percentage point
of organic matter in the top six inches of soil can hold roughly 20,000
additional gallons of water per acre.

This is the intersection of two pages: the soil-building work described in
[Whole-system farming](whole-system-farming.md) is also water infrastructure.
A farm that builds its organic matter from 2% to 5% over a decade has added
roughly 60,000 gallons of water-holding capacity per acre -- capacity that
reduces irrigation needs, buffers drought, and slows runoff.

## Responsible irrigation

When supplemental water is needed, drip irrigation delivers water directly to
the root zone of each plant, with very little lost to evaporation or runoff.
A timer and a moisture sensor together can ensure that water is applied only
when the soil actually needs it, and only as much as the roots can use.

Mantra records each irrigation event: date, duration, volume, and which field
or bed received it. Combined with moisture sensor readings and harvest
records, these data show whether the irrigation is serving the crop well or
whether adjustments would help.

## Constructed wetlands

Water that leaves a farmstead -- from a kitchen, a wash station, a processing
area -- carries nutrients and organic matter. A **constructed wetland** is a
shallow, planted basin that treats this water biologically before it returns
to the landscape.

The principle is simple: water flows slowly through a bed of gravel and
aquatic plants. The plants and the microorganisms on their roots consume the
nutrients and organic matter, and what emerges at the far end is clean water
suitable for irrigation or for returning to a stream.

A constructed wetland is infrastructure that treats water the way compost
treats crop residues -- by putting biology to work. It closes the water loop
the same way composting closes the nutrient loop.

## What the machine tracks

A Grain machine keeps the water story:

- Rainfall (date, amount, recorded from a gauge or a weather station)
- Tank levels (daily or weekly, recorded manually or from a sensor)
- Irrigation events (date, duration, volume, location)
- Soil moisture readings (date, depth, location)
- Wetland performance (inlet and outlet quality, when tested)

Over years, these records show the farm's water balance -- how much arrives as
rain, how much the soil holds, how much is applied as irrigation, and how much
leaves through the wetland. A farm whose soil holds more water each year is a
farm growing its own resilience, and the record proves it.

## Something to try

The next time it rains, put a straight-sided cup or jar outside in the open.
After the rain stops, measure the depth of water in the cup with a ruler.
That depth, in inches, is the rainfall for that storm.

Now look at a roof near you and estimate its area in square feet. Multiply
the area by the rainfall depth by 0.623. The result is how many gallons that
roof could have captured from that single storm. The number is often larger
than people expect, and it is the beginning of thinking about water as a
resource to catch rather than a problem to drain away.
