## What this page teaches

Moving food from a farm to the people who eat it takes energy. This page
describes rail as the most efficient way to move agricultural products over
land, how small-scale rail infrastructure could serve regional food systems,
and how Grain's records support the coordination that shared infrastructure
requires.

## Why rail

Moving one ton of freight one mile takes roughly:

- **A truck:** about 150 watt-hours
- **A train:** about 50 watt-hours

Rail is roughly three times more efficient than road transport for the same
weight over the same distance. For agricultural products -- heavy, not
urgent, moving in bulk from growing regions to population centers -- that
efficiency matters.

Rail also concentrates its impact. A rail line occupies a narrow corridor.
A highway system occupies a wide one, with parking, interchanges, and the
land alongside them that changes character because of the traffic. For a
farming region, the land a highway takes is land that could have been
growing food.

## Regional food rail

Most rail infrastructure today serves long-distance freight or passenger
traffic. A regional food system needs something smaller:

- **Short lines.** Ten to fifty miles, connecting a cluster of farms to a
  regional distribution point -- a food hub, a processing facility, a
  wholesale market.

- **Scheduled service.** Regular pickup days, the way a mail route runs.
  A farm knows that Tuesday's harvest reaches the hub by Wednesday morning.

- **Shared infrastructure.** The rail line, the loading facilities, and the
  rolling stock are shared among the farms and food businesses in the
  region. No single farm needs to own a train.

- **Cold chain.** Refrigerated cars that keep produce at the right
  temperature from farm to hub. The energy cost of refrigeration on rail
  is lower than on road, because the insulated volume is larger and the
  transit time is shorter.

This is not science fiction. Short-line railroads exist throughout North
America, many of them underused. Reviving them for regional food
distribution is an infrastructure question, not a technology question.

## What the records coordinate

Shared infrastructure needs shared records. A Grain machine at a farm, at a
hub, and at a distribution point can each keep its own Mantra log, and the
three logs together describe the chain:

- **The farm's log** records what was harvested, when, and what was loaded
  for pickup.

- **The hub's log** records what arrived, from which farm, in what
  condition, and where it was directed next.

- **The distribution point's log** records what reached the customer and
  closes the chain.

Each log is append-only and sealed. A dispute about a damaged shipment has
three independent records to consult rather than one party's word against
another's. A certification inspector can trace a product from field to
market through records that were never editable after the fact.

## Something to try

Look up the short-line railroads in your region. Many states and provinces
maintain a map of active rail lines. Find the ones nearest to agricultural
land and ask: if this line carried food instead of (or in addition to) what
it carries now, which farms could it reach? Which population centers could
it serve?

The distance between the answer and the present reality is an infrastructure
gap, and infrastructure gaps are the kind of problem that gets solved when
enough people see the same map.
