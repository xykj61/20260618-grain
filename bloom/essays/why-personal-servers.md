## What this essay teaches

This essay explains why Grain runs on a machine you own rather than on
a service you rent. It is written for a farmer, a cooperative manager,
or a small business owner who has been told that "the cloud" is the
only modern choice -- and who deserves to hear the other side of that
story honestly.

## What a cloud service is

When you use a cloud service, your data lives on someone else's
computer. You access it through the internet. The company that owns
the computer charges you a monthly fee, and in exchange they keep the
machine running, handle backups, and provide the software.

This arrangement works. Millions of businesses use it. Yet it comes
with conditions that are worth understanding before you sign up.

## The conditions

**You pay forever.** A cloud subscription is rent. You pay every month
for as long as you use the service. If you stop paying, you lose
access to your own records. A machine you own costs money once. It
sits on your shelf, it runs your software, and it does not send you
a bill in January.

**The terms can change.** A cloud service has terms of service -- a
legal agreement that says what the company may do with your data, how
much the service costs, and what happens if either side wants to end
the arrangement. The company can change those terms. They usually
notify you, and you usually accept, because moving your records to
another service is difficult enough that most people stay.

**The service can end.** Companies go out of business, get acquired,
or decide that your market segment is not profitable enough to serve.
When that happens, your records need a new home on a timeline you did
not choose.

**Your data travels.** When your records live on someone else's
machine, they travel over the internet to get there. They pass through
networks you do not control. They rest on hardware you have never
seen, in a data center whose location you may not know.

## What a personal server is

A personal server is a computer that belongs to you, sits in your
home or your office, and runs software you control. Your records live
on it. Your network connects to it. Nobody else has the keys.

Grain is designed to run on a personal server. The machine can be as
simple as a small single-board computer -- inexpensive, quiet, and
energy-efficient. It connects to your local network and to the
internet when you choose.

## What you gain

**Ownership.** Your records are yours. They sit on your hardware, in
your building, under your control. No subscription, no terms of
service, no third party with access to your data.

**Stability.** A machine on your shelf does not go out of business. The
software that runs on it does not change unless you change it. Your
records from five years ago are in the same place, in the same format,
accessible the same way.

**Privacy.** A farm's records contain business information -- what you
grow, how much you sell, who your customers are, what your costs look
like. On a personal server, that information stays in your building.

**Independence.** You are not locked into one company's decisions about
pricing, features, or continued service. If the software needs to
change, the source code is open and a community technician can modify
it.

## What it costs

Honesty requires naming the costs too.

**You maintain it.** A cloud service handles maintenance for you. A
personal server needs occasional attention -- updates, backups, and
the rare hardware replacement. Grain is designed to make this
maintenance as simple as possible, yet it is not zero.

**You provide the hardware.** A small server costs money to buy.
It costs electricity to run -- though a modern single-board computer
uses less power than a light bulb.

**You provide the network.** The server needs a network connection.
For a farm with internet access, this is the connection you already
have.

## Why it matters for agriculture

A farm's records are the memory of the land. Soil tests, planting
dates, harvest weights, compost applications, water measurements --
these records accumulate over years and decades. They are worth more
as they age, because patterns visible over ten years are invisible
over one.

Trusting those records to a service that charges rent and can change
its terms is trusting the memory of your land to someone else's
business model. A personal server keeps that memory where it belongs
-- on the farm, with the farmer, for as long as the farm stands.

## Something to try

Look at the cloud services your household or business pays for.
Add up the monthly cost. Multiply by ten years. That number is the
rent you are paying for the privilege of storing your own information
on someone else's machine.

Now price a small single-board computer. The difference is the
arithmetic of ownership, and it runs in one direction.

*A farm that owns its records owns its memory. A machine on the shelf
is a quiet companion -- it asks for nothing, it forgets nothing, and
it belongs to the person who put it there.*
