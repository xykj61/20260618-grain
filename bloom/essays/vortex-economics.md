## What this essay teaches

This essay describes how money moves through a local food economy --
where it enters, where it circulates, and where it leaves. It
introduces the concept of a local multiplier and explains how
transparent records make the circulation visible. It is written for
anyone who buys or sells food locally and wants to understand the
economics underneath.

## How money circulates

When you buy tomatoes from a local farm, your money enters the local
economy. The farmer uses part of that money to buy seeds from the
local feed store. The feed store owner uses part of their income to
eat at the local restaurant. The restaurant buys vegetables from
local farms. Each transaction keeps the money circulating within
the community.

This circulation is the local multiplier. A dollar spent locally
can generate several dollars of economic activity before it leaves
the community. The exact number depends on how many transactions
happen locally before the money is spent elsewhere.

## Where money leaves

Money leaves the local economy -- economists call this leakage --
when it is spent on goods or services from outside the community:

- **Imported food.** When a grocery store buys tomatoes from a
  distant supplier instead of a local farm, the purchase price
  leaves the community immediately.

- **National chains.** A purchase at a national chain sends most of
  the revenue to a corporate headquarters elsewhere. The local
  store pays local wages and local rent, yet the profit leaves.

- **Distant suppliers.** A farm buying seeds from a multinational
  seed company sends that money out of the community. A farm buying
  seeds from a regional seed producer keeps more of it local.

- **Fuel and energy.** Money spent on fuel and electricity flows to
  energy companies, most of which are not local. A farm generating
  its own energy -- solar, wind, biogas -- reduces this leakage.

Leakage is not inherently wrong. No community is self-sufficient,
and trade with the outside world brings goods and services a
community cannot produce for itself. Yet understanding where money
leaves helps a community decide where local alternatives would
strengthen the local economy.

## The multiplier in practice

Consider two scenarios for a community's food spending:

**Scenario A: Imported food.** A household spends 100 dollars at a
national grocery chain. The chain pays 10 dollars in local wages
and sends 90 dollars to its supplier network and corporate office.
The local employee spends their 10 dollars locally. Total local
economic activity: roughly 110 dollars. Multiplier: about 1.1.

**Scenario B: Local food.** A household spends 100 dollars at a
local farm stand. The farmer spends 40 dollars on local supplies,
labor, and services. Those recipients spend a portion locally as
well. Total local economic activity: roughly 200 to 250 dollars.
Multiplier: about 2.0 to 2.5.

These numbers are illustrative rather than precise -- the actual
multiplier depends on the community and its supply chains. Studies
of local food systems consistently find multipliers between 1.5
and 3.0, compared to 1.0 to 1.5 for conventional food supply
chains. The pattern is real even when the exact numbers vary.

## Making the circulation visible

Most of this circulation is invisible. A farmer knows what they
sold and what they bought. A restaurant knows the same. Yet nobody
sees the whole picture -- how much money entered the local food
economy this month, how many times it circulated, and where it
left.

This is where transparent records change the picture. When farms,
processors, distributors, and retailers keep records in Grain's
Dimeroll ledger, the transactions become visible in aggregate:

- **Revenue by source.** How much of the cooperative's income came
  from local buyers versus distant ones?

- **Spending by destination.** How much of the cooperative's
  spending went to local suppliers versus distant ones?

- **Circulation depth.** How many local transactions did a dollar
  pass through before it left the community?

These are not abstract statistics. They are the answers to questions
a community asks when it decides where to invest -- in a shared
cold storage facility, in a local seed producer, in a delivery
route that connects farms to schools.

## A regional food system as economic development

A regional food system is an economic development strategy. It
creates jobs, it keeps money circulating locally, and it builds
infrastructure that serves the community for decades.

The argument is not that every community should be self-sufficient
in food. It is that every community that grows food is already an
economic engine, and understanding the circulation helps that engine
run more effectively.

A farm that sells directly to local buyers keeps more revenue per
pound than a farm that sells through a distant distributor. A
processing facility that turns local tomatoes into local sauce
adds value that stays in the community. A delivery route that
connects local farms to local institutions -- schools, hospitals,
senior centers -- keeps food dollars circulating where the food
was grown.

Grain's records make this visible. The Dimeroll ledger tracks the
transactions. The Mantra log tracks the physical movement. Together,
they show not just what happened, but where the value went.

## Something to try

The next time you buy groceries, sort your receipt into two
columns: items from within 100 miles, and items from farther away.
Estimate how much of each dollar stays in your community versus
how much leaves immediately.

The exercise takes five minutes and the answer is usually
surprising. The gap between the two columns is the opportunity
a local food system exists to close.

*Money is like water -- it flows where the channels take it. A
local food economy builds channels that keep the water circulating
through the community's own fields, kitchens, and tables before
it flows away. The records are what make the channels visible,
so the community can choose where to deepen them.*
